\chapter{The MQTT / Sparkplug~B contract}
\label{ch:contract}

This chapter is the versioned integration contract of record between \prog and Ignition:
the topic grammar, the metric definitions, quality/freshness semantics, and the
lifecycle (NBIRTH/NDATA/NDEATH).

\begin{note}
Everything in this chapter describes behaviour that is \emph{implemented and tested} as of
Epic~4. Where the bridge does not yet do something the Sparkplug specification allows, it
is listed in section~\ref{sec:contract-limits} rather than described as though it worked.
\end{note}

\section{Contract version}

The node BIRTH carries a metric \code{Contract/Version} (\code{Int64}), currently
\textbf{12}. \emph{(This sentence said \textbf{8} until 2026-08-22, while the bridge emitted
10 --- a stale constant in a document, which the chapter's own mechanical guard does not check
because it reads routes and cause slugs, not numbers.)} It is incremented on \emph{any} change to the topic grammar, to a metric name or
unit, or to the meaning of a published quality code. Publishing it means a consumer can
\emph{see} the contract change instead of having to notice it from broken tags.

Since version~4 the number is also \emph{enforced}: \code{tests/contract\_golden.rs} pins the
quality codes and the cause vocabulary to the version they belong to, and fails if either
moves without this number moving. Before that, the number was maintained by whoever
remembered.

The number does not say what \emph{kind} of change it was, so it is worth stating. Version~12
is \textbf{breaking}, and it replaces version~11 rather than extending it. Version~11 declared a
\code{Cause} \emph{property} in every BIRTH so that Ignition would materialise it, which it did
--- and the evening's measurement showed the other half: \textbf{a metric property is written by
a BIRTH and by nothing else}. A \textsc{ddata} carrying a new value for it is ignored, while the
same message's quality update is applied. So the property stood frozen at its birth value, which
meant \code{no-reading-yet} beside a healthy meter, for as long as the session lived. Under
version~10 an operator saw nothing, which is uninformative; under version~11 they saw something
untrue. From version~12 the cause travels as \emph{two metrics}, \code{Cause/Power} and
\code{Cause/Energy} --- a \code{Cause} folder holding two string tags, whose values are what a
\textsc{ddata} exists to change --- and the property is gone. Version~11
is \textbf{breaking}, and it is the first version whose reason is a \emph{host} behaviour rather
than a change of judgement. Ignition materialises a metric property only when a BIRTH declares
it: a property that first appears in a \textsc{ddata} is discarded. So contract~4's \code{Cause}
--- the whole point of which is that an operator can see \emph{why} a value is not good --- never
reached anyone, because a meter that is healthy at its birth and degrades later publishes its
cause in \textsc{ddata} only. Measured against a live Ignition on 2026-08-21 and confirmed on
2026-08-22 on a group the host had never seen. From this version the property is declared in
every BIRTH and published in \emph{every} message, a \textsc{good} metric included, where it
carries the explicit value \code{no-cause}. Version~10
is \textbf{additive}: one new cause, \code{device-not-in-account}, says the account itself
pronounced the configured device id absent --- smart-me's own \code{404}, which until this
version travelled under \code{configuration-contradicted}. It is split out the way version~8
split the refusals: the repair site differs (the meter row, or the account the device left ---
not the file's plumbing), and it is the one latch that is evidence about the \emph{device},
which is why the bridge answers it with a DDEATH where the other latches say nothing about
theirs. One \code{404} covers a mistyped id and a removed device alike; the bridge cannot tell
them apart and its message says so rather than guessing. Version~9
was also additive: one new cause, \code{feed-not-advancing}, says that the \emph{cloud} stopped
rebuilding its answer --- two consecutive successful fetches carried the same \code{Date} header.
It is the only cause in the vocabulary that cannot be produced by looking at a single reading:
every other judgement is a fact about one response, this one is a relation between two. A replayed
response is internally consistent, so its age looks plausible and every other check passes it,
which is why a frozen cloud was indistinguishable from a working one until this version. It
degrades rather than latching --- a frozen feed may thaw, and the meter behind it may be perfectly
well. Version~8
is \textbf{additive}: four more causes, three of which split \code{source-refused} into the
refusals an operator repairs in different places --- a rejected credential, a configuration
smart-me contradicts, and a meter answering under the wrong serial. \code{source-refused}
survives, narrowed to the one case none of the three describes: a meter latched by a refusal that
already happened. The fourth, \code{source-rate-limited}, is the first source failure carrying an
\emph{instruction} rather than a diagnosis. Version~7
is \textbf{additive}: four new causes join the vocabulary a \code{Cause} property can carry,
listed under \emph{Why a value is not good} below. What they replace is not another cause but an
\emph{absence} of one --- \code{value-unusable} was published for all four faults at once and
named no field, so an operator was sent to look for a meter problem when smart-me's unit contract
had moved. It survives with its meaning narrowed to one case, not a single usable number in the
whole reading, so a consumer keyed on it still sees it: less often, and more precisely. Version~6
is \textbf{breaking}: a verdict now belongs to a \emph{metric} rather than to the reading, so a
fault in the energy index no longer withholds the power value beside it. Nothing is renamed and no
tag is added --- but a consumer that recorded \code{Power = null} whenever the energy index was
refused records a real value for the identical situation from this version on, and points from
either side cannot be compared without knowing which side they are on. The change is a correction;
a correction that alters what a stored point \emph{means} is still breaking. Version~5
is \textbf{additive}: one new cause, \code{counter-went-backwards}, joins the vocabulary a
\code{Cause} property can carry, so a consumer sees a value it had not seen before on a
property it already held. Version~4
is \textbf{additive}: every metric that is \emph{not} good now carries a \code{Cause}
property naming which check refused it, so a consumer sees one new property on degraded
metrics and nothing it already held became wrong. Version~3
is \textbf{additive}: the birth certificate gained the \code{Node Control/Rebirth} metric, so
a consumer sees one new tag and nothing it already held became wrong. Version~2 was
\textbf{breaking}: a quality code a consumer had recorded as good was, from that release on,
not good, and values from either side of that boundary cannot be compared without knowing
which side they are on. Both bump, and both should --- but only one of them invalidates
history.

\section{Topic grammar}

The namespace is \code{spBv1.0}. One EON node represents the bridge; each meter is a
\emph{device} beneath it, identified by its immutable serial number.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l>{\ttfamily}l@{}}
\toprule
\normalfont\textbf{Level} & \normalfont\textbf{Topic} \\
\midrule
Node   & spBv1.0/\{group\}/\{TYPE\}/\{node\} \\
Device & spBv1.0/\{group\}/\{TYPE\}/\{node\}/\{serial\} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\code{group} and \code{node} are configured; \code{serial} is the meter's serial number,
never a friendly name. Binding the topic to the serial is what makes it impossible to
attribute a value to the wrong meter after a device is renamed or replaced.

Identifiers are validated \emph{before} the bridge connects. A serial that cannot be a
topic level makes the bridge refuse to publish, because the alternative — connecting,
never birthing, and publishing nothing — looks identical to a healthy but idle bridge.

\subsection{Message types emitted}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\ttfamily}l l p{7.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
\normalfont\textbf{Type} & \textbf{Level} & \textbf{When} \\
\midrule
NBIRTH & node   & On every successful connect, including reconnects. \\
DBIRTH & device & Immediately after NBIRTH, one per meter. \\
DDATA  & device & On each poll that produced a reading. \\
DDEATH & device & When a meter is \emph{disabled}, mid-session, under the same
                  \code{bdSeq}. The node is untouched: no NDEATH, no rebirth.
                  Enabling one publishes a DBIRTH the same way. \\
NDEATH & node   & On shutdown (published by the bridge) and on connection loss
                  (published by the broker as the last will). \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\code{NDATA} is \emph{not} emitted: the bridge carries no node-level measurements.

\begin{note}
\textbf{DDEATH became live on 2026-08-04} (story~5.2) and this chapter said the opposite
until then --- *``a device only stops reporting when its node does''*. It was true while a
meter could only be switched off by stopping the bridge; the moment a meter could be
disabled on its own, it stopped being true, and the sentence survived the change. The
specification permits a DBIRTH mid-session provided the NBIRTH went out in the same session
(\code{tck-id-message-flow-device-birth-publish-nbirth-wait}), and \emph{requires} a DDEATH
whenever a device can no longer provide real-time information --- which a meter you have just
disabled cannot.
\end{note}

\section{Quality and freshness}
\label{sec:quality}

Quality travels as a \textbf{metric property} under the key \code{Quality}, typed
\code{Int32}, not as a value — a consumer that ignores properties still reads a number, and
one that reads them learns whether to trust it.

The code is a 32-bit pattern in which the \textbf{level lives in the top bits} and the low
16 bits are a subcode:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l r r p{4.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Quality} & \textbf{Subcode} & \textbf{Sent as (Int32)} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\midrule
\textsc{good}  & 192 & \code{192}         & Fresh, trusted value. \\
\textsc{stale} & 516 & \code{-2147483132} & Previously valid, no longer current
                                            (\emph{Bad\_Stale}). \\
\textsc{bad}   & 512 & \code{-2147483136} & Unusable: read failure or failed
                                            validation (\emph{Bad}). \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Why a value is not good}

Since contract~4, a metric whose quality is \emph{not} \textsc{good} carries a second
property, \code{Cause}, typed \code{String}, naming the check that refused it ---
\code{reading-too-old}, \code{source-unreachable}, \code{counter-went-backwards} and so on.

\textbf{Since contract~12 the cause is a TAG of its own}, \code{Cause/Power} or
\code{Cause/Energy}, beside the measurement it describes --- not a property on it. A property is
written by a BIRTH and never updated afterwards, measured against a live Ignition, so a cause
carried as one froze at its birth value and went on asserting a fault the meter had recovered
from. The tag reads \code{no-cause} when the measurement is good, and it is itself
\textsc{good} even when the measurement is not: it states what the bridge judged, which is
always known, and a tag that explains a fault must not become unreadable exactly when it matters.

\textbf{Since contract~11, a \textsc{good} metric names its absence of cause too, reading
\code{no-cause}.}
Until then it carried nothing, on the reasoning that a reason published beside every good value
is noise a consumer learns to ignore. That reasoning still holds --- and it lost to a host
behaviour. Ignition materialises a metric property only when a \textsc{birth} declares it, so
the property has to be declared before anything is wrong; and once a host holds a tag property,
it holds the \emph{last value it was sent}. A bridge that fell silent on the tick a meter
recovered would leave \code{reading-too-old} standing beside a healthy number, indefinitely.
\code{no-cause} is louder than silence and it is the only one of the two that cannot become a
lie.

\begin{note}
\textbf{The cold start says \code{no-reading-yet}, not \code{no-cause}.} The two metrics a
device declares before its first poll are \textsc{stale}, so answering \emph{no cause} there
would be false. Until contract~11 they were the only non-good pair this bridge published that
named no reason at all --- an operator seeing \code{Bad\_Stale} seconds after a start could not
tell a fresh bridge from a broken feed. Now they say which it is.
\end{note}

\begin{note}
\textbf{Since contract~7, a cause names the field it is about.} A payload can fail in four
ways the bridge tells apart, and each is repaired somewhere else:
\code{unit-not-recognised} (smart-me's unit contract moved --- read their documentation),
\code{value-not-finite} (a \textsc{nan} or an infinity arrived where a measurement was owed ---
the device or the cloud), \code{value-overflowed} (a finite input became non-finite in
\prog's own rescale --- ours), and \code{source-timestamp-unparseable} (the numbers may be
perfect and we cannot say \emph{when} they were true --- a freshness fault, not a value one).
All four were published as \code{value-unusable} before, which named no field and sent an
operator to the wrong place three times out of four. \textbf{And the refusal lands on the
field alone}: an unreadable power unit no longer withholds the energy index beside it.
\end{note}

\begin{note}
\textbf{The reason cannot live in \code{Quality}, and the specification is what decides
that.} \code{tck-id-payloads-propertyset-quality-value-value} admits only \code{0},
\code{192} and \code{500} in that property. \prog already deviates there deliberately (see
the warning below); encoding a reason as a fourth value would deepen a deviation accepted
only because the alternative was a silent lie. A separate key costs nothing: a
\code{PropertySet} constrains only that keys and values are equal in number.
\end{note}

\begin{note}
\textbf{A counter that goes backwards is \textsc{bad}, not \textsc{stale}} (contract~5, and
since contract~6 the refusal lands on the \code{Energy} metric alone). The
value may be perfectly current --- a replacement meter really does read 12~kWh --- and it is
the \emph{relation} to the previous reading that is broken. \textsc{bad} publishes null
values, which withholds exactly the number a consumer would difference; publishing it
\textsc{stale} would hand the number over with a hint. The drop is reported once and the new
index becomes the reference, so a replaced meter is not marked bad for ever.
\end{note}

\textsc{stale} deliberately uses the same code a host raises when a node's DEATH marks its
tags stale. Transport-level and application-level staleness then look identical to the
consumer, which is what the two-mechanism design promises: one visible outcome, whichever
mechanism noticed.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{These codes deviate from the Sparkplug B specification, deliberately.} The
specification admits exactly three quality values —
\code{0}, \code{192}, \code{500} — but Ignition does not classify the property by that
enumeration: it reads the raw integer as its own quality code, in which the \emph{level} sits
in the top bits. Measured on Ignition~8.3.7, \code{500} displays as \emph{Good(500)} and
\code{0} as \emph{Good\_Unspecified}. Two of the three specified codes therefore report an
unusable value as trustworthy on the host this bridge publishes to.

Between conforming and not lying, \prog does not lie. The deviation is recorded in ADR~0012
and in \code{docs/sparkplug-conformance.md}; the generic \code{sparkplug-b} library keeps the
specified codes, so only this bridge deviates, and only for this consumer.

Contract version~1 published the specified codes and was, for that reason, displaying stale
data as good.
\end{warning}

\subsection{Timestamps are the meter's, never the bridge's}

Every published metric is stamped with the \emph{source} measurement time
(\code{ValueDate}), never with the moment the bridge published it. Freshness is therefore
computed end-to-end, and a message lost in transit reads downstream as old data rather
than as current data.

\subsection{The first message is honest}

A cold-start device BIRTH declares the tag set with \textbf{no value} and quality
\textsc{stale}. Before the first successful fetch the bridge genuinely has nothing to
report, and declaring a placeholder zero would be indistinguishable from a meter reading
zero.

\subsection{A meter that stops answering says so}

When a poll fails --- the cloud is silent, the credentials are refused, the deadline elapses
--- the bridge republishes that meter's \textbf{last known value} carrying the new quality,
stamped with that value's own \code{ValueDate}. It does \emph{not} fall silent.

The reason is that silence on a Sparkplug wire is not a statement. It is indistinguishable
from ``nothing has changed'', so a host goes on displaying the last value it received, at the
quality it received it with. A meter that had published \textsc{good} and then stopped would
have been shown as good indefinitely --- which is precisely the failure this bridge exists to
prevent. It behaved that way until 2026-08-07.

A meter that has \emph{never} answered is a different case and gets nothing: its device BIRTH
already declared it valueless and \textsc{stale}, which is the truth, and inventing a reading
to carry a quality would be the same lie facing the other way.

\subsection{A rebirth never promotes a value}

The bridge re-declares its tags on a reconnect, and also whenever a host requests a rebirth
(section~\ref{sec:contract-ncmd}). Both take the same path, so what follows holds for both.
Re-declared readings are degraded
(\textsc{good} becomes \textsc{stale}, never the reverse) and stamped with the reading's
own \code{ValueDate}. Without this, a 45-minute outage would come back looking like a
fresh measurement.

\section{Delivery semantics}

Every edge-node message \prog \emph{publishes} goes out with \textbf{retain false}, and at
\textbf{QoS~0} except for the death certificates, which ride at \textbf{QoS~1}. Retain is
the important half: a retained payload is a value the broker replays to any future
subscriber with no way to judge its age --- a stored lie that outlives the process.
Freshness is the BIRTH's job, not the broker's.

\begin{note}
\textbf{The specification does not say one thing for every message.} QoS~0 is required for
NBIRTH, DBIRTH, \textbf{NDATA and DDATA} (\code{tck-id-payloads-nbirth-qos},
\code{\dots-dbirth-qos}, \code{\dots-ndata-qos}, \code{\dots-ddata-qos}, each reading
\emph{``MUST be published with the MQTT QoS set to 0''}); QoS~\textbf{1} for the will
(\code{\dots-will-message-qos}, repeated at
\code{tck-id-payloads-ndeath-will-message-qos}). \textbf{Retain false is mandated for those
same five}, by the \code{-retain} sibling of each clause. Only DDEATH and an
\emph{explicitly} published NDEATH carry no QoS or retain clause at all, so those two are
\prog's decisions rather than requirements.

\textbf{Corrected 2026-08-11.} This paragraph previously claimed the norm was silent on
NDATA and DDATA. It is not, and the sentence it replaced --- written before Story~4.17 ---
was accurate. \prog's behaviour was never affected: those messages already went out at
QoS~0. What was wrong was the \emph{account} of why, and an account that calls a MUST a
preference is what lets a later edit move it in good conscience.
\end{note}

\begin{note}
\textbf{The will was a genuine violation until 2026-08-10, and it is fixed.} \prog
registered its will at QoS~0 like everything else, and a unit test pinned that value ---
so the test locked in the violation and any repair turned it red. Story~4.17 replaced both:
the will now registers at QoS~1, and the test states the mandated rows and the chosen rows
separately, each mandated row citing the clause that fixes it.

Why it was not cosmetic: at QoS~0 the broker performs no acknowledgement, no retry and no
queueing, so a will could simply be lost --- and the death certificate is the one message
whose loss produces exactly the frozen-value-shown-as-live failure this chapter exists to
prevent.
\end{note}

\textbf{The explicit death certificate rides at QoS~1 with the will}, and that is \prog's
\emph{choice}. It was justified here by \code{tck-id-\dots-death-payload}, which the
2026-08-11 review found to be
\code{tck-id-operational-behavior-host-application-death-payload} --- a clause about the
\emph{Host Application}'s STATE will, in JSON, and not about an edge node at all. Nothing
in the norm makes the shutdown certificate byte-identical to the registered will. The
choice is still the right one, on its own merits: both are built from the same message
type, and a lost death produces exactly the frozen-value-shown-as-live failure this chapter
exists to prevent.

\textbf{DDEATH remains at QoS~0}, and the asymmetry is deliberate but unresolved: a lost
DDEATH strands one device on the host exactly as a lost NDEATH strands the node.
Harmonising them is a decision in its own right and was not the violation Story~4.17 was
written to fix.

\begin{note}
QoS~0 defines no acknowledgement, so the bridge cannot and does not claim a message was
\emph{delivered}. It reports a value as published only once the transport accepted it for
transmission, and a value it could not hand over produces a per-device traced drop rather
than silence. See ADR~0010.
\end{note}

\section{Session lifecycle}

\subsection{Boot order}

The order is not negotiable, and it is why the death certificate is built before anything
connects:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item restore \code{bdSeq} from disk and open the next session;
  \item serialise the NDEATH certificate for that session;
  \item register it as the connection's last will \emph{inside} the CONNECT packet;
  \item connect;
  \item subscribe to the node's \code{NCMD} topic;
  \item publish NBIRTH, then one DBIRTH per meter.
\end{enumerate}

Get this wrong and the broker holds no will while the node is alive: the bridge dies and
nobody is told.

Steps 5 and 6 are in that order because the specification requires it --- the subscription
must exist \emph{before} the NBIRTH goes out, not merely in the same sequence. A consumer
that answers a birth certificate with a command would otherwise be talking to a node that
is not listening yet. Both steps run on \emph{every} connect, so the ordering holds after a
reconnect as well as at boot.

\code{bdSeq} is persisted across restarts through an atomic write (write-temp, fsync,
rename), so a power loss mid-write cannot leave a corrupt session number.

\subsection{Commands are received; one is honoured}
\label{sec:contract-ncmd}

\prog subscribes to

\begin{center}\code{spBv1.0/<group>/NCMD/<node>}\end{center}

\noindent at \textbf{QoS~1}, on every connect, before publishing its birth certificate.
This is the one place where \prog uses a QoS other than~0; the rule that everything is
published at QoS~0 governs \emph{publishing}, and a subscription QoS is a different setting
travelling the other way.

\textbf{One command is honoured: \code{Node Control/Rebirth}.} It is the command the
specification requires every edge node to implement, and it is the only one \prog acts on.
Everything else that arrives is logged and discarded.

A rebirth request is recognised by its \emph{name and its value together}: a metric named
\code{Node Control/Rebirth} carrying the boolean value \code{true}. The specification
defines it that way, and the value half matters --- \code{false} is what \prog's own
\code{NBIRTH} declares, so a host echoing that declaration back is not asking for anything.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.3cm} p{8.3cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{What arrives} & \textbf{What \prog does} \\
\midrule
\code{Node Control/Rebirth} = \code{true}
                         & \textbf{Answers it.} Republishes the complete birth sequence ---
                           one \code{NBIRTH} followed by one \code{DBIRTH} per meter --- and
                           logs it at \code{INFO}. The session is \emph{not} reopened: the
                           \code{bdSeq} is unchanged, because a rebirth re-announces a
                           session rather than starting one. No measurement is published
                           between the request and the last \code{DBIRTH}. \\
\code{Node Control/Rebirth}, any other value
                         & \textbf{Does not answer, and says so loudly.} Logged at
                           \code{WARN} \emph{with the datatype and value exactly as they
                           arrived}. This line is deliberate: if some host encodes the
                           request differently from what \prog accepts, this is what makes
                           that visible instead of silent. A request that is quietly not
                           recognised looks identical to a host that never asked. \\
A command naming other metrics & Logs the metric \emph{names} at \code{INFO} and discards
                           it. Names only --- a full payload dump would be noise and could
                           carry values. \\
Bytes that do not decode & Logs at \code{WARN} and discards. Anyone can publish to this
                           topic, so a malformed payload is an ordinary event and never
                           stops the bridge. \\
A command with no metrics & Logged too, distinctly, so it cannot be confused with a
                           command whose names were lost. \\
Any command with the MQTT \emph{retain} flag set
                         & \textbf{Refused as a replay}, and logged at \code{WARN} naming
                           the flag. A retained command is one the broker kept and handed
                           back on subscribing, not one a host is sending now; acting on it
                           would re-announce the node on every reconnect for as long as the
                           retained message existed. The specification requires commands to
                           be published without retain, so no conforming host sends one. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{note}
There is no rate limit on rebirth answers and no coalescing of a burst. A host may resend;
each request is answered. The reasoning is that a birth here is small --- one \code{NBIRTH}
plus one \code{DBIRTH} per configured meter --- while a \emph{suppressed} answer is
invisible to the host, which cannot tell it from a node that never heard. The inbound
command queue is bounded and a full queue is a logged drop, so a burst degrades to some
answers plus visible drops, never to a stall.
\end{note}

A rebirth is also the one thing that can make \prog publish a birth without having
reconnected. The log line says which of the two happened, because the causes to go looking
for are entirely different.

\prog also reads the broker's answer to its own subscription, and says so in the log. If
the broker \emph{refuses} the subscription --- an ACL, typically --- that is logged at
\code{ERROR} naming the topic; if it grants a QoS lower than~1, that is logged at
\code{WARN}. Neither stops the bridge: publishing without a command path is better than
not publishing. This matters because a refused subscription is not an error and not a
disconnect; without this check it would look exactly like a topic nobody publishes on.

There is a third, rarer outcome, and it is not the broker's doing. If \prog cannot even
\emph{queue} the subscription --- its outbound request queue is full at the instant the
connection is accepted --- it logs that at \code{ERROR} and then births anyway, running that
session with no command path and without retrying. The choice is deliberate, and it is the
same one as everywhere else in this chapter: a bridge that publishes is worth more than one
that refuses to. But it means an \code{ERROR} line about the subscription is the only sign,
and it is a different line from the broker's refusal. All log output requires no
configuration: \prog logs at \code{INFO} and above by default, and \code{RUST\_LOG}
overrides that if you want more or less.

\begin{note}
\textbf{Why subscribe at all if nothing is honoured?} Because the ignoring is the part that
has to be safe. A subscription that silently fails, a payload that crashes the bridge, or a
command applied by halves are each worse than no subscription; none of them are visible
without the diagnostics above. The subscription is also mandatory in the specification
regardless of what a node does with the messages. Command handling itself is planned
work --- see \emph{Known limitations}.
\end{note}

\subsection{Shutdown --- both deaths fire}
\label{sec:contract-shutdown}

On \code{SIGTERM} the bridge publishes an explicit NDEATH, keeps the transport pumping
until it reaches the wire, and then \textbf{drops} the connection. It never sends a clean
MQTT DISCONNECT, because that instructs the broker to \emph{discard} the will.

The consequence is visible on the wire and worth expecting: a consumer sees
\textbf{two NDEATH messages} for one graceful stop, carrying the same \code{bdSeq} ---
the bridge's own certificate first, then the broker's will when the socket closes at
process exit. Duplicate deaths are idempotent for a consumer that has already marked the
node down.

\begin{note}
Requiring the explicit certificate, rather than relying on the will alone, is a decision
recorded in ADR~0011. The will only fires once the broker concludes the connection is
dead; a half-open connection --- a container network torn down without a FIN, a firewall
dropping the flow silently --- stays invisible until the keep-alive elapses, which at the
configured 30~seconds means up to 45~seconds of a dead node displayed as live. That is
acceptable for an unplanned death and not for a planned stop.
\end{note}

The two mechanisms are complementary rather than alternative: the will remains the
\emph{only} mechanism for a hard death --- crash, \code{SIGKILL}, power loss.

\section{Known limitations}
\label{sec:contract-limits}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Only one command is honoured}: \code{Node Control/Rebirth}, which the
        specification requires of every edge node. \prog subscribes to its \code{NCMD}
        topic, receives every command sent there, and acts on that one --- see
        section~\ref{sec:contract-ncmd}. Any other command is logged and discarded. In
        particular there is no \code{DCMD} path at all, so nothing can be written to a
        device output; that is a separate capability with its own safety argument, and it
        does not exist yet.
  \item \textbf{Anyone who can reach the broker can request a rebirth.} The broker on this
        installation requires no credentials, so any client may publish a rebirth request
        and \prog will answer it. It is not a new exposure: the same client could already
        publish a forged death certificate, which lies immediately, whereas a forged rebirth
        republishes what is already true. Two consequences are worth knowing about, and
        neither is worth a mechanism here --- the correct control is broker-side, with
        credentials or an ACL on the \code{NCMD} topic.

        \textbf{Answering a rebirth costs a stale window.} A rebirth re-declares every
        reading \prog is holding, and it re-declares them \emph{degraded}: a value that was
        \code{Good} is republished as \code{Bad\_Stale}. That is deliberate and it is the
        safe direction --- the reading has not been re-fetched, so it cannot honestly be
        re-asserted as good, and \prog never publishes a fresh-looking value it has not
        verified. But it means every tag reads stale from the moment the rebirth is answered
        until the next poll completes, up to one polling interval later. A host that asks for
        rebirths repeatedly can therefore hold every tag at \code{Bad\_Stale} indefinitely.
        No measurement is lost and nothing is misreported; the data simply declares itself
        untrustworthy, which is what it is.

        \textbf{A \emph{retained} rebirth request is refused.} MQTT lets a publisher ask the
        broker to keep a message and replay it to everyone who subscribes afterwards. Since
        \prog subscribes on every connect, a retained rebirth request would be handed to it
        again on every reconnect, for as long as the retained message existed --- with nobody
        sending anything, and a log line indistinguishable from a real host asking. \prog
        therefore refuses a command that arrives with the retain flag set and logs it as a
        replay, naming the flag and how to clear it. This costs nothing: the specification
        requires commands to be published \emph{without} retain, so no conforming host ever
        sends one. A retained message is cleared by publishing an empty payload, retained, to
        the same topic.
  \item \textbf{DDEATH rides at QoS~0 while NDEATH rides at QoS~1.} Neither is mandated,
        but a lost DDEATH strands a device exactly as a lost NDEATH strands the node, so
        the asymmetry is unresolved rather than justified. See \emph{Delivery semantics}.
        (Replaces the will's own QoS~0 violation, closed 2026-08-10 by Story~4.17.)
  \item \textbf{No \emph{Primary Host Application} support --- and since 2026-07-31 this is
        a \emph{decision}, not a gap.} Sparkplug lets an Edge Node
        name a host and change its behaviour with that host's \code{STATE}: wait for it
        before birthing, disconnect when it goes offline, walk to another broker. \prog
        implements none of this --- it births as soon as the broker answers, whether or
        not any consumer is listening, and never reads a \code{STATE} topic.

        \textbf{It was ruled out deliberately}, in \textbf{ADR 0018} (\emph{Primary Host /
        \code{STATE} is ruled out; the repair path is host-initiated}), on four
        grounds: the specification states that \emph{``specifying a Primary Host is not
        required for an Edge Node''}; without store-and-forward the wait preserves
        \emph{zero} readings, so it buys honesty rather than data; one broker means the
        stranding the mechanism guards against cannot occur; and implementing it would add
        a \textbf{never-births} state --- which was the real state of this broker on
        2026-07-28, when no retained \code{STATE} existed at all. The ADR also records the
        conditions that would reopen it: a second broker, store-and-forward, or
        report-by-exception (which would remove the trigger the repair below depends on).

        \textbf{This is \emph{not} invisible on a single-broker installation, and an
        earlier edition of this manual wrongly said it was.} The cost appears when the
        host restarts. \prog's \code{NBIRTH} was published once, at QoS~0 and not
        retained, so the broker keeps no copy for a returning consumer; \prog reads no
        \code{STATE} topic, so it never learns the host came back; and its own broker
        session is untouched by the host restarting, so \emph{that} event prompts nothing.
        \textbf{The protocol's own repair for this now works.} It is an \code{NCMD}
        \code{Node Control/Rebirth}, and \prog both receives it and answers it --- the
        request reaches the bridge and the bridge republishes its complete birth sequence.
        A returning host that notices it is receiving data for a node whose definitions it
        does not have can therefore ask for them and get them, without anything happening
        on \prog's side and without waiting.

        That is the important change, and it is worth being exact about what it does and
        does not fix. The repair is \emph{host-initiated}: it works if and only if the host
        asks.

        \textbf{Whether MQTT Engine asks \emph{on its own} has not been observed}, and an
        earlier edition of this manual asserted that it does. What was measured is that
        Engine sends a rebirth request when an operator presses the control, and that
        \prog answers it correctly. The evidence that it might also ask unprompted is
        indirect: Ignition exposes a \emph{Rebirth (Last) Cause} field that read
        \emph{``Triggered by user''}, a label that only earns its existence if other causes
        exist. That is an inference, not a measurement, and settling it is question~1 on
        Story 4.8's list.

        \prog still reads no \code{STATE} topic, so it never learns the host came back, and
        it will not volunteer a birth. A host that does \emph{not} ask still sees
        \code{NDATA} it has no \code{NBIRTH} to interpret.

        \textbf{Any reconnect on \prog's own side also restores the definitions.} A fresh
        \code{NBIRTH} is published on every successful MQTT connection, so a broker
        restart, a network interruption or a keep-alive expiry all repair the consumer's
        view --- see the first item in this list. Restarting \prog is the most direct
        remedy, but it has not been the only one for some time, and an earlier edition of
        this paragraph wrongly said it was. What no event on the \emph{host} side can do is
        prompt that reconnect by itself --- which is why the rebirth path above matters.

        Redundant-broker stranding is a second, separate consequence, and it does require
        a multi-broker topology.
  \item \textbf{\code{bdSeq} is incremented per CONNECT}, as the specification requires
        --- since 2026-08-01. It was previously fixed for the lifetime of one MQTT client,
        because the underlying library reconnects internally and rebuilds the CONNECT
        packet from the options it was constructed with, so the registered will could
        never be updated. \prog{} now owns its own reconnect loop: each CONNECT builds a
        new client and registers a \emph{new} will carrying the session number that
        CONNECT will use.

        The order matters and is worth stating, because getting it half right is worse
        than the old behaviour: advancing the number \emph{without} re-registering the
        will would leave the broker holding a certificate for a session that no longer
        exists, and a consumer pairing death to birth by \code{bdSeq} would discard it
        and keep a frozen value on screen, presented as live.
  \item \textbf{\code{bdSeq} is persisted once per CONNECT} (it was once per boot until
        2026-08-01), and a missing or
        corrupt file restarts the numbering at the sentinel rather than refusing to start.
  \item \textbf{No report-by-exception}: an unchanged \code{ValueDate} republishes the
        same point on every poll.
  \item \textbf{Broker TLS is off} pending an upstream dependency upgrade; the broker is
        expected on a trusted local network. Traffic to the smart-me cloud is unaffected
        and remains TLS-only.
\end{itemize}

\section{The closed metric list}
\label{sec:metriclist}

\textbf{Two metrics, per device, and no others.} \prog publishes exactly:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\ttfamily}l l l@{}}
\toprule
\normalfont\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Unit, declared in the BIRTH} \\
\midrule
Power  & \code{Double} & kW \\
Energy & \code{Double} & kWh \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The list is closed in the strong sense: it is two constants in the publisher, and adding a
third would change what a host discovers at birth --- a contract change, governed by the
section below. \textbf{Both are 64-bit doubles}, including the cumulative energy counter: a
32-bit float loses whole kilowatt-hours once a counter passes a few million, silently.

\textbf{The device list is your configuration}, one Sparkplug device per enabled meter, keyed
by serial (\S\ref{sec:mappingscheme}). Disabling a meter publishes a \code{DDEATH} for it;
enabling one publishes a \code{DBIRTH}. Nothing appears on the wire that is not a meter you
configured and confirmed.

\begin{note}
\textbf{A metric whose quality is not \emph{Good} carries its cause}, from a closed vocabulary
of twenty-one. That vocabulary is \emph{part of the contract}: a consumer reads it off the
metric, so adding a cause is a contract change and the golden test refuses one that arrives
without a version bump.
\end{note}

\section{How \code{CONTRACT\_VERSION} is governed}
\label{sec:contractgovernance}

\code{CONTRACT\_VERSION} is a single integer published in every BIRTH. It answers one question
for a consumer: \emph{may what I stored yesterday be compared with what arrives today?}

\textbf{It is bumped whenever the answer is no.} In practice:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{5.6cm} l p{4.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Change} & \textbf{Bump?} & \textbf{Why} \\
\midrule
A metric added or removed & \textbf{yes} & what a host discovers at birth changes \\
A unit changed & \textbf{yes} & every stored point means something else \\
A cause added to the vocabulary & \textbf{yes} & a consumer reads causes off degraded metrics \\
The meaning of a published value changed --- including a \emph{correction} & \textbf{yes} & a
correction that alters what a stored point means is still breaking. Calling it additive
because the new behaviour is better is how a consumer gets surprised \\
A new optional field a consumer can ignore & no & nothing already stored changes meaning \\
Anything that never reaches the wire --- screens, logs, the configuration file & no & the
contract is what is published \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{note}
\textbf{The rule is enforced by a test, not by discipline.} \code{contract\_golden} holds a
recorded shape of the published contract, cause vocabulary included, and fails when the live
one differs from the golden for the declared version. It has caught a bump that was owed
before any human noticed --- which is the only reason to write a governance section at all: a
procedure nothing enforces is a paragraph.
\end{note}

\begin{warning}
\textbf{The version is not the software's version.} \prog's release number moves with every
change; \code{CONTRACT\_VERSION} moves only when what is on the wire stops being comparable.
Several releases share one contract version, and that is the point of having two numbers.
\end{warning}
